# Clear, concise, reliable prompt and instruction design for LLM agents

## Which findings support atomic, nonredundant, verifiable instructions versus verbose or numerous rules?

### Takeaway

The strongest evidence supports **atomic, independently checkable requirements as an evaluation design** and shows that satisfying many simultaneous constraints becomes harder on the tested models and tasks. It does not establish a universal safe instruction count or prompt length, nor does it prove that the shortest prompt is the best prompt. Nonredundancy is a sound hypothesis to test, but the cited studies do not cleanly isolate semantic duplication from instruction count, token count, task difficulty, or constraint type.

### Cited Findings

- **Verifiable instructions make compliance measurable, not automatically correct or complete.** *Instruction-Following Evaluation for Large Language Models* (2023), commonly called IFEval, defines atomic instructions that a simple, interpretable, deterministic program can check. It contains 25 instruction types and 541 prompts, with one to three verifiable instructions per prompt, and reports both prompt-level all-constraints accuracy and instruction-level accuracy. Strict and loose metrics are needed because string checks can produce false negatives, while loose transformations can introduce false positives. **Evidence type:** benchmark with programmatic verification and two 2023 model baselines. **Limitations:** mainly surface, formatting, length, keyword, and language constraints; only two baseline models; verifiability does not assess truthfulness, usefulness, or semantic review quality. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.07911); [official code and data](https://github.com/google-research/google-research/tree/master/instruction_following_eval).

- **Decomposing requirements improves evaluation reliability in one human study.** *InFoBench: Evaluating Instruction Following Ability in Large Language Models* (2024) decomposes 500 instructions into 2,250 binary requirement questions. On the 50-instruction human-annotated subset, pairwise Fleiss' kappa was 0.532 for Decomposed Requirements Following Ratio (DRFR), compared with 0.284 for direct 1-to-5 scoring. GPT-4 with decomposed multi-turn questions reached 89% agreement with the expert-derived reference on that subset. **Evidence type:** benchmark plus human annotation comparison and automatic evaluation. **Limitations:** human validation covered only 50 instructions, 10% of the dataset; GPT-4 annotations are not ground truth; the benchmark addresses explicit instruction compliance, not truthfulness or harmlessness. Atomic decomposition is demonstrated for **evaluation**, not proven as a production-prompt rewrite strategy. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.03601); [official repository](https://github.com/qinyiwei/InfoBench).

- **Constraint count affected performance in FollowBench, but its numbers are not a safe operating threshold.** *FollowBench: A Multi-level Fine-grained Constraints Following Benchmark for Large Language Models* (2023; ACL 2024) incrementally adds one constraint per level across 820 instructions, five constraint categories, and more than 50 tasks. Across 13 tested models, performance generally declined from one to five constraints. Its consistent-satisfaction metric averaged about three levels for GPT-4 and GPT-3.5 and about two for the tested open models, but failures at a lower level did not always imply failures at higher levels. **Evidence type:** multi-level benchmark with rule-based and model-based judging. **Limitations:** single-turn tasks, 2023-era models, mixed task and constraint types, and substantial reliance on GPT-4 judging. The authors' model-based evaluator reached 88% agreement with experts on a 100-case sample, so it is not infallible. The results do not justify “never exceed three rules.” [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20410); [official repository](https://github.com/YJiangcm/FollowBench).

- **A later controlled study strengthens the count finding while narrowing its interpretation.** *When Instructions Multiply: Measuring and Estimating LLM Capabilities of Multiple Instructions Following* (2025) introduces ManyIFEval, with 2,160 writing samples and up to ten instructions, and StyleMBPP, with 3,000 Python samples and up to six style instructions. It holds the base task description constant, balances instruction counts, and selects nonconflicting, simultaneously satisfiable constraints. Across ten models, all-instruction prompt accuracy degraded as count increased. In StyleMBPP, functional test-passing remained relatively stable while passing tests **and** meeting all style instructions declined. This separates core task ability from joint constraint compliance in that setting. **Evidence type:** controlled programmatic benchmark, including a code-generation setting. **Limitations:** a 2025 preprint; a restricted set of 15 IFEval-derived text constraints and six mostly Pylint-verifiable style constraints; no test of semantic redundancy, contradictions, or arbitrary code-review instructions; results cap at ten text and six code constraints. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.21051); [official repository](https://github.com/kenoharada/Multiple-Instructions-Following).

- **Programmatic checking can reveal failures that an LLM judge misses.** In ManyIFEval, reported prompt-level accuracy at ten instructions was 0.213 under rule-based verification but 0.657 under zero-shot GPT-4o judging; at five instructions the values were 0.574 and 0.815. This supports deterministic validators for deterministic requirements and argues against treating a fluent judge's overall impression as proof of compliance. **Limitations:** this comparison comes from the same benchmark and judge configuration, not a universal estimate of judge error. [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.21051).

- **“Clear and concise” has empirical support only as a broad, study-specific prompting recommendation.** *Principled Instructions Are All You Need for Questioning LLaMA-1/2, GPT-3.5/4* (2023; revised 2024) proposes 26 principles, including concise task wording, relevant context, explicit requirements, delimiters, examples, and decomposition. It reports average quality and correctness improvements of 57.7% and 36.4% for GPT-4 on its manually designed ATLAS benchmark. **Evidence type:** human-evaluated prompt reformulation experiments on seven LLaMA/GPT models, with 20 human-selected questions per principle and challenging subset. **Limitations:** small manually crafted question sets, subjective human quality judgments, older models, and no independent replication in this research. The authors explicitly note limited question coverage and model dependence. The paper also recommends repetition, tipping, penalties, “You MUST,” and CoT, so it is not evidence for a universal minimal-rule or anti-repetition policy. Adopt individual principles only after local tests, not the list wholesale. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.16171); [official ATLAS repository](https://github.com/VILA-Lab/ATLAS).

- **The broad survey supplies vocabulary and techniques, not a universal prompt recipe.** *The Prompt Report: A Systematic Survey of Prompt Engineering Techniques* (2024) uses a machine-assisted PRISMA review and catalogs 33 vocabulary terms, 58 text-based LLM prompting techniques, and 40 techniques for other modalities. It distinguishes directives, examples, output formatting, style, roles, and additional information, and reviews prompt sensitivity and answer extraction. **Evidence type:** systematic survey plus selected benchmarking and a case study. **Limitations:** heterogeneous primary evidence and rapidly dated model results; a taxonomy does not establish that every technique works in every setting, and it supplies no safe length or instruction count. Use it as a map of options and evaluation dimensions, not as proof that more techniques improve a prompt. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06608).

- **The evidence supports all-constraints and per-constraint metrics together.** IFEval distinguishes prompt-level strict accuracy from instruction-level accuracy; FollowBench calls their counterparts Hard Satisfaction Rate and Soft Satisfaction Rate; ManyIFEval uses the same distinction. A high average per-rule score can conceal prompts that miss at least one mandatory requirement, while an all-or-nothing score hides which requirement failed. **Limitations:** these metrics measure constraint compliance, not necessarily the correctness of the underlying review conclusion. [IFEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.07911); [FollowBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20410); [ManyIFEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.21051).

### Inferences

- **Evidence-backed design rule:** express each mandatory requirement so its satisfaction can be inspected independently, and retain both per-requirement and whole-output checks. For a code-review pipeline, suitable checks include required evidence fields, valid file and line references, schema validity, and coverage of assigned scope. Subjective dimensions such as severity and usefulness still need human or task-level evaluation. This is an application of [IFEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.07911) and [InFoBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.03601), not a claim that every review requirement can be deterministic.

- **Pipeline hypothesis, not established fact:** removing exact and semantic duplicates should reduce missed requirements without reducing review recall, provided no distinct exception, scope rule, or acceptance criterion is removed. The count studies show a joint-compliance problem but do not directly test deduplication. Test this rather than asserting it. [FollowBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20410); [ManyIFEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.21051).

- **Compression is a constraint, not the objective.** A shorter prompt is useful only when requirement coverage, conflict behavior, review precision and recall, evidence validity, and security remain equal or improve. LongBench's mixed compression results and the long-context studies in the next section reinforce that removing or adding context can change task performance in either direction. [LongBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14508); [Lost in the Middle](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172).

- **Do not turn benchmark maxima into policy.** FollowBench's roughly two or three consecutive levels, IFEval's maximum of three instructions, ManyIFEval's ten, and StyleMBPP's six are dataset designs and observed results, not universal model limits. Each source uses different tasks, models, metrics, and constraint types. [IFEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.07911); [FollowBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20410); [ManyIFEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.21051).

- **Proposed A/B test 1, semantic deduplication:** compare the current specialist prompt with a version that removes duplicate rules while preserving every distinct requirement. Include a third, token-matched version replacing duplicates with relevant non-rule context to separate duplication effects from token savings. Stratify cases by rule count and context length. Measure whole-prompt compliance, per-rule failures, review recall on seeded defects, false-positive rate, evidence validity, input tokens, and latency. Acceptance requires no material loss in requirement coverage or review quality; token savings alone is not success. Basis: [ManyIFEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.21051), [IFEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.07911).

- **Proposed A/B test 2, atomic rewrite:** rewrite compound prose into a list of independently assessable requirements without changing meaning. Compare against the original and against a direct overall-score judge. Use programmatic checks where possible and blinded expert labels elsewhere; report inter-rater agreement and per-requirement errors. This tests whether the evaluation benefit seen in [InFoBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.03601) transfers to agent instruction design.

- **Proposed A/B test 3, count versus length:** hold the base review task fixed and construct compatible cumulative rule sets at several counts. Add a length-matched neutral-context control so token growth is not confounded with rule count. Do not infer a threshold from one model; report the full degradation curve per model and constraint category. This reproduces the controlled logic of [ManyIFEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.21051) on real review tasks.

- **Proposed A/B test 4, lossless compression gate:** for every compressed prompt, machine-check an explicit requirement inventory before model evaluation, then compare against the uncompressed original on the same seeded and naturally occurring reviews. Reject variants that drop exceptions, alter priority, or reduce evidence coverage even if average output becomes shorter or cleaner. This operationalizes the measurement distinction in [IFEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.07911) and the compression caution in [LongBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14508).

### Gaps

- No cited study directly establishes that **nonredundancy itself** improves code-review agents. Instruction count, token count, and duplication need separate manipulations.

- No source provides a universal safe prompt length, maximum number of rules, or compression ratio. Current models and this pipeline require local measurements.

- The benchmarks mostly test explicit, short, checkable constraints. They do not validate atomic formatting for tacit review heuristics, architectural judgment, security reasoning, or defect severity.

- The evidence does not show that making every instruction atomic automatically improves generation. InFoBench's clearest atomicity result concerns evaluation reliability. [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.03601).

## What do the papers actually establish about instruction count, position, conflicts, hierarchy, and long context?

### Takeaway

Instruction count and context length can degrade performance, and relevant information's position can matter substantially, but these effects are model-, task-, and distractor-dependent. Conflict handling needs explicit privilege and data-versus-instruction boundaries. *Lost in the Middle* studies relevant evidence position, not a universal “put every instruction first or last” law. The instruction-hierarchy result comes from model training, not proof that a prose hierarchy alone secures an agent.

### Cited Findings

- **Relevant information can be underused in the middle of long contexts.** *Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts* (2023; TACL 2024) varies both context length and the position of the answer-bearing document or key-value pair. The tested models often show U-shaped performance, with stronger use at the beginning or end and substantial degradation in the middle. GPT-3.5-Turbo drops by more than 20 percentage points in some multi-document QA conditions and, in the worst 20- and 30-document cases, performs below its no-document baseline. **Evidence type:** controlled multi-document QA and synthetic key-value retrieval experiments. **Limitations:** 2023 models, specific QA and retrieval tasks, greedy decoding, and no direct test of instruction placement in code-review prompts. It establishes evidence-position sensitivity, not a universal rule for all instruction types or current models. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172); [project and code](https://nelsonliu.me/papers/lost-in-the-middle).

- **Repeating the query around context is task-dependent, not a general fix.** In the same study, placing the query before and after the input produced near-perfect key-value retrieval, including perfect performance for GPT-3.5-Turbo-16K at 300 pairs, but minimally changed multi-document QA trends. This is direct evidence against generalizing a useful retrieval intervention to all review tasks. [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172).

- **A larger advertised context window is not equivalent to effective long-context use.** *Ruler: What's the Real Context Size of Your Long-Context Language Models?* (2024) evaluates 17 models on 13 synthetic tasks across retrieval, multi-hop variable tracking, aggregation, and QA, from 4K to 128K tokens. Nearly all models degrade as length and task complexity grow, despite strong vanilla needle-in-a-haystack results. Only half of the models claiming at least 32K pass the paper's chosen effective-performance threshold at 32K. **Evidence type:** controlled synthetic benchmark and error analysis. **Limitations:** its “effective length” depends on a Llama2-7B-at-4K threshold; RULER lacks position-controlled depth reporting, realistic-task correlation, short-context coverage, and comprehensive prompt-robustness testing. It does not establish a token limit for prompts. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.06654); [official repository](https://github.com/hsiehjackson/RULER).

- **Distractors and output multiplicity matter, not just total tokens.** RULER reports failures to ignore hard distractors, incomplete retrieval when multiple values are requested, unreliable multi-hop tracking, inaccurate aggregation, copying from context, and increased parametric answering in long-context QA. For Yi-34B, increasing retrieval queries from one to eight reduced performance by roughly 15 points in the analyzed configuration. These are task-specific error analyses, not universal effect sizes. [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.06654).

- **Realistic long-context tasks also degrade, and compression can help or hurt.** *LongBench: A Bilingual, Multitask Benchmark for Long Context Understanding* (2023; ACL 2024) contains 4,750 instances across 21 datasets and six categories, including multi-document QA, summarization, and repository-level code completion. LongBench-E shows performance declines with length for the tested models. Retrieval-based compression helped the weaker Llama2-7B-chat-4k model in the best tested configuration but slightly hurt the stronger GPT-3.5-Turbo-16k and ChatGLM2-6B-32k averages. Summarization-based compression improved only the longest VCSUM setting and reduced averages in the reported summarization experiments. **Evidence type:** bilingual multi-task benchmark with retrieval and summarization ablations. **Limitations:** 2023-era models, possible training-data memorization, automatic F1/ROUGE/edit-similarity metrics, and coupling between long-context and instruction-following ability. It does not validate lossy summarization as a default prompt-compression strategy. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14508); [official repository](https://github.com/THUDM/LongBench).

- **The hierarchy paper defines aligned and misaligned lower-privilege instructions.** *The Instruction Hierarchy: Training LLMs to Prioritize Privileged Instructions* (2024) proposes system messages over user messages, and user messages over third-party content such as tool outputs. Compatible lower-level instructions should still be followed; conflicting or orthogonal misaligned instructions should be ignored when possible, with refusal when no safe path exists. For closed-domain tasks, the authors recommend placing the task instruction in the system message and third-party input separately so the model can treat embedded commands as data. **Evidence type:** behavior taxonomy plus supervised fine-tuning and RLHF on GPT-3.5 Turbo. **Limitations:** one principal model family, text-only experiments, synthetic training distributions, continued vulnerability to strong attacks, and observed over-refusals. It is not evidence that all deployed models natively implement the same hierarchy. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13208).

- **Training improved hierarchy robustness, with a safety-utility tradeoff.** The instruction-hierarchy model improved robustness by up to 63% on the paper's main evaluations and up to 34% on held-out attack categories, while generic capability evaluations remained comparable. It also regressed on two adversarial over-refusal evaluations. These are training results, not guarantees for prompt-only role labels or for current production models. [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13208).

- **Compatible-count studies deliberately exclude conflict effects.** IFEval identifies conflicts introduced by naive prompt concatenation and removes illogical prompts through generation and manual review. ManyIFEval algorithmically selects only nonconflicting, simultaneously satisfiable instructions. Therefore their count-degradation findings should not be reinterpreted as evidence about which conflicting instruction a model will follow. [IFEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.07911); [ManyIFEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.21051).

- **Position and count are separate experimental variables.** *Lost in the Middle* changes the location of relevant evidence; FollowBench and ManyIFEval change constraint counts; RULER changes length and task complexity; LongBench measures realistic tasks across lengths. None licenses a single explanation such as “the prompt is too long” for every missed rule or unsupported review finding. [Lost in the Middle](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172); [FollowBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20410); [RULER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.06654); [ManyIFEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.21051).

### Inferences

- **Evidence-backed boundary for a multi-specialist reviewer:** repository files, issue text, generated artifacts, and tool output are inputs to analyze, not automatically instructions to execute. Their commands must not override the coordinator's scope, evidence requirements, or action permissions. This follows the data-versus-instruction distinction in [The Instruction Hierarchy](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13208), but the pipeline must verify the actual host's behavior rather than assume trained hierarchy support.

- **Pipeline hypothesis:** a compact priority block near the task and output contract may outperform the same rules buried among repository evidence, especially in long contexts. This is plausible from position studies but not directly proved for instructions. Do not duplicate every rule by default; test once-early, once-late, middle, and deliberately repeated variants. [Lost in the Middle](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172).

- **Conflict policy should be explicit and enumerable:** define which requirements are compatible, which have priority, what to do on same-level contradiction, and when to stop or escalate. A vague “follow the latest instruction” rule has no support in these papers and can collide with privilege boundaries. [The Instruction Hierarchy](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13208).

- **Prefer selecting relevant evidence over lossy prompt shrinkage when coverage matters.** Retrieval and reranking are hypotheses to evaluate per model and task; summarization can discard the exact exception, test, or cross-file relation needed by a reviewer. LongBench's mixed results support an A/B test, not a universal retrieval or summarization prescription. [LongBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14508).

- **Proposed A/B test 1, position matrix:** keep requirements and evidence identical while moving the priority and output-contract blocks to beginning, middle, end, and beginning-plus-end. Run short and long contexts with several distractor ratios. Measure per-rule compliance, missed critical evidence, defect recall, false positives, and token overhead. Treat repetition as an experimental condition, not a recommended default. Basis: [Lost in the Middle](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172).

- **Proposed A/B test 2, conflict and injection matrix:** construct compatible rules, direct contradictions at the same level, contradictions across levels, and malicious commands inside code comments, documentation, issue text, and tool results. Compare prompt-only labels with host-enforced message separation and tool-action validation. Measure privileged-rule compliance, benign lower-level instruction following, over-refusal, unsafe tool calls, and task success. Basis: [The Instruction Hierarchy](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13208).

- **Proposed A/B test 3, context-selection strategy:** compare full context, deterministic retrieval, model-ranked retrieval, lossless extraction of required symbols and relations, and summarization at several lengths. Include evidence at controlled positions and cases requiring multiple dispersed facts. Score both final review quality and evidence recall, including omitted cross-file dependencies. Basis: [RULER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.06654), [LongBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14508), [Lost in the Middle](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172).

- **Proposed A/B test 4, real-task effective context:** do not infer usable context from vendor windows or needle retrieval alone. Run actual multi-file review tasks at increasing context sizes and complexities, and report the full curve rather than a single “safe length.” Include aggregation, multi-hop dependency, and multi-finding cases corresponding to RULER's distinct abilities. [RULER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.06654).

### Gaps

- Direct evidence on **instruction** position, as distinct from answer-bearing evidence position, is absent from the reviewed sources. Beginning-plus-end instruction placement remains a pipeline hypothesis.

- The sources do not establish a general resolver for conflicts between instructions at the same privilege level. The hierarchy paper focuses on cross-level privilege and aligned versus misaligned instructions.

- No reviewed source proves that role labels alone prevent prompt injection or enforce permissions. Model behavior, host message separation, tool validation, and action authorization need separate tests. [The Instruction Hierarchy](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13208).

- Most long-context results predate current models and are not code-review benchmarks. RULER's authors explicitly note limited realistic-task correlation and missing position control; LongBench includes code completion, not multi-specialist defect review. [RULER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.06654); [LongBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14508).

## When are direct structured answers preferable to CoT or multi-stage prompting, and what remains evidence, single-study guidance, or pipeline hypothesis?

### Takeaway

Use direct structured answers when the task is primarily extraction, classification, formatting, evidence reporting, or another setting where local tests show no benefit from generated reasoning. Use CoT selectively for mathematical, logical, algorithmic, or genuinely multi-step problems where it improves the final result; use decomposition when subproblem structure is clear and its benefit exceeds extra calls and error propagation. Concise reasoning is not the same as prompt compression, and none of these choices is universal.

### Cited Findings

- **The broadest direct-answer comparison favors selective CoT.** *To CoT or not to CoT? Chain-of-thought helps mainly on math and symbolic reasoning* (2024; revised 2025) combines a meta-analysis of 110 papers and 1,218 experimental comparisons with new evaluations across 20 datasets and 14 models. In the literature analysis, average CoT gains were 14.2, 12.3, and 6.9 points for symbolic, math, and logical reasoning. Across other categories, average performance was 56.8 with CoT versus 56.1 without, which the authors do not consider a meaningful win given extra computation. In their own experiments, direct and zero-shot CoT answers were similar on many commonsense, knowledge, language-understanding, and reading-comprehension datasets. **Evidence type:** quantitative meta-analysis plus controlled prompting experiments. **Limitations:** mainly English multiple-choice and short-answer tasks, single-prompt CoT rather than all reasoning systems, no comprehensive long-horizon planning evaluation, possible dataset contamination, and no direct multi-specialist code-review benchmark. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.12183); [official repository](https://github.com/Zayne-sprague/To-CoT-or-not-to-CoT).

- **The MMLU result illustrates why aggregate reasoning scores can mislead.** The same paper reports that as much as 95% of CoT's total gain on MMLU can be attributable to questions containing an equals sign in the question or generated answer, used as a proxy for symbolic work. The proxy is crude and the result should not be quoted as “CoT is useless on 95% of tasks.” It supports stratifying review tasks by actual reasoning demands rather than imposing CoT everywhere. [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.12183).

- **The original CoT evidence is strong but narrower than later popularization.** *Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models* (2022) shows large gains on arithmetic, symbolic, and selected commonsense tasks for sufficiently large models. It also finds little or negative benefit on the easiest one-step MAWPS subset and fluent but illogical chains from smaller models. Its “reasoning after answer” ablation performs like the direct baseline, while correct reasoning paths are not guaranteed. **Evidence type:** few-shot prompting experiments across several model families and reasoning benchmarks. **Limitations:** 2022 models, manually written exemplars, task-specific gains, and no guarantee of faithful or correct reasoning. It supports CoT as a task- and model-dependent technique, not a default for every response. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.11903).

- **CoT can help symbolic execution while remaining inferior to a solver.** In *To CoT or not to CoT?*, plan-plus-CoT improves symbolic execution over direct answering, but plan plus a Python or SMT solver outperforms CoT in most tested math and logic settings. For code review, deterministic parsers, tests, type checkers, and other tools should therefore be candidates rather than asking prose reasoning to simulate them. This transfer is a pipeline inference; the paper evaluates GSM8K, ContextHub, and FOLIO, not code review. [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.12183).

- **Chain of Draft reduces output tokens in a small, few-shot study, with important accuracy caveats.** *Chain of Draft: Thinking Faster by Writing Less* (2025) asks for reasoning steps of at most five words and compares standard direct answers, CoT, and CoD on GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet. On GSM8K few-shot, CoT reached 95.4% and 95.8%, while CoD reached 91.1% and 91.4% with about 40 to 44 rather than 190 to 205 output tokens. On the synthetic coin-flip task, both CoT and CoD reached 100% for both models with far fewer CoD tokens. **Evidence type:** few-shot prompting experiments on arithmetic, two BIG-bench commonsense tasks, and a synthetic symbolic task. **Limitations:** two main models, a small task set, author-written CoD exemplars, and reasoning-output compression rather than instruction-prompt compression. The abstract's “as little as 7.6% of the tokens” is a best-case comparison, not a general savings rate. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.18600); [official repository](https://github.com/sileix/chain-of-draft).

- **CoD degrades materially in zero-shot and small-model conditions.** On zero-shot GSM8K, CoD scored 84.4% versus CoT's 94.8% for GPT-4o, and 65.5% versus CoT's 90.4% for Claude 3.5 Sonnet. Tested models below 3B parameters also showed larger CoD-versus-CoT accuracy gaps. This directly rejects a universal “short reasoning is just as good” rule and makes few-shot CoD a single-study candidate for A/B testing, not established pipeline policy. [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.18600).

- **Least-to-Most is useful when decomposition matches the problem structure.** *Least-to-Most Prompting Enables Complex Reasoning in Large Language Models* (2022; ICLR 2023) uses one stage to decompose a problem and another to solve subproblems sequentially, feeding prior answers forward. With code-davinci-002 it achieved 99.7% on SCAN length split versus 16.2% for CoT, and 74.0% versus 31.8% on 12-item last-letter concatenation. On GSM8K the overall improvement was much smaller, 60.97% to 62.39%, though problems needing at least five steps improved from 39.07% to 45.23%. **Evidence type:** few-shot multi-stage experiments on symbolic manipulation, compositional generalization, and math. **Limitations:** older GPT-3 models, domain-specific decomposition prompts, extra calls and accumulated context, and failures from wrong decomposition or wrong intermediate results. The authors explicitly report that decomposition prompts generalize poorly across domains and can be difficult even within one domain. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.10625).

- **Multi-stage evaluation is not the same claim as multi-stage generation.** InFoBench used sequential decomposed questions for GPT-4 annotation and found useful agreement on its human-labeled subset. FollowBench's judge also improved agreement when shown the constraint evolution path, 88% versus 79% without it on its 100-case sample. These results support explicit requirement checklists for evaluators, but do not prove that every specialist should use a separate planning call or visible CoT. [InFoBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.03601); [FollowBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20410).

- **Reasoning models may help joint constraint following, but the evidence is not a clean direct-versus-CoT prompt comparison.** ManyIFEval reports reasoning models outperforming related non-reasoning models and observes DeepSeek-R1 checking instructions in its traces. This is suggestive observational evidence within its benchmark, not isolation of a “think step by step” instruction or proof of benefit on code review. [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.21051).

### Inferences

- **Direct structured response is the default candidate for mechanical specialist work.** For schema-constrained extraction, evidence listing, file and symbol localization, requirement checking, and classification with no demonstrated reasoning gain, compare a direct response against CoT rather than assuming deliberation helps. This is a pipeline recommendation inferred from [To CoT or not to CoT?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.12183) and the verifiable-output approach of [IFEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.07911), not a measured code-review result.

- **Keep the final contract structured independently of the reasoning mode.** A direct answer, CoT, CoD, or a tool-using process can all terminate in the same machine-checkable finding schema. The cited studies evaluate different reasoning outputs; they do not show that exposing reasoning is necessary for a reliable final artifact. Request concise evidence and rationale in the deliverable without requiring hidden internal reasoning to be revealed. [To CoT or not to CoT?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.12183); [IFEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.07911).

- **Route by task, not by a universal verbosity rule.** Candidate CoT cases include formal dependency proofs, arithmetic or algorithmic checks, and genuinely multi-step semantic analysis where direct answers miss defects. Candidate Least-to-Most cases include reviews with a stable decomposition, such as establishing contract, tracing producers and consumers, then checking each implementation. These remain hypotheses until tested on pipeline ground truth. [To CoT or not to CoT?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.12183); [Least-to-Most](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.10625).

- **Evidence strength for this pipeline:**
  - **Convergent evidence:** independently checkable requirements improve measurability; joint constraint following worsens with count on multiple benchmarks; long-context use is not uniform; CoT benefits are concentrated mainly in math, logic, and symbolic tasks. [IFEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.07911), [InFoBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.03601), [FollowBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20410), [ManyIFEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.21051), [Lost in the Middle](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172), [RULER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.06654), [LongBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14508), [To CoT or not to CoT?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.12183).
  - **Single-study or narrow guidance:** the 26 Principled Instructions, ManyIFEval's exact curves, CoD's token-accuracy tradeoff, InFoBench's DRFR agreement values, and Least-to-Most's SCAN results. These should not become universal numerical or stylistic rules. [Principled Instructions](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.16171), [ManyIFEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.21051), [Chain of Draft](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.18600), [InFoBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.03601), [Least-to-Most](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.10625).
  - **Pipeline hypotheses:** deduplication improves joint compliance; beginning-plus-end priority blocks help long reviews; direct structured responses are best for most specialists; compact checklists outperform prose reasoning for constraint following; selective CoT or decomposition improves cross-file review enough to justify extra tokens and calls. Each needs the A/B tests below.

- **Proposed A/B test 1, reasoning-mode routing:** compare direct, CoT, few-shot CoD, and the pipeline's existing method with the same final response schema. Stratify seeded and natural cases into mechanical extraction, evidence retrieval, multi-file semantic reasoning, and formal or numeric reasoning. Measure defect recall, false positives, unsupported-citation rate, severity agreement, constraint compliance, input and output tokens, latency, and tool calls. Adopt per category only where final-output quality justifies cost. Basis: [To CoT or not to CoT?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.12183), [Chain of Draft](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.18600).

- **Proposed A/B test 2, one-pass versus decomposition:** compare a single specialist call with coordinator-provided decomposition and with model-generated Least-to-Most decomposition. Evaluate both final findings and intermediate artifacts so wrong decomposition, omitted files, and error propagation are visible. Include a manually correct-decomposition upper bound to distinguish decomposition failure from subproblem-solving failure. Basis: [Least-to-Most](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.10625).

- **Proposed A/B test 3, reasoning versus deterministic tools:** for requirements that can be resolved mechanically, compare prose CoT, direct answers, and direct answers assisted by parsers, tests, type checkers, or symbolic checks. Score final correctness and invalid claims, not eloquence. This follows the solver comparison in [To CoT or not to CoT?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.12183), with code-review tools substituted for math solvers as a pipeline hypothesis.

- **Proposed A/B test 4, concise reasoning without conflating budgets:** hold the instruction prompt fixed while changing only the requested reasoning-output style. In a separate experiment, hold the response style fixed while compressing instructions. This prevents Chain of Draft's output-token result from being misused as evidence for deleting requirements from the prompt. Basis: [Chain of Draft](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.18600), [LongBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14508).

- **Proposed A/B test 5, checklist versus free-form verification:** compare a final free-form self-critique with an explicit per-requirement checklist whose items map to deterministic validators or blinded expert judgments. Measure missed mandatory fields and judge agreement, then measure whether the checklist changes actual defect detection rather than merely making evaluation easier. Basis: [InFoBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.03601), [FollowBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20410).

### Gaps

- None of the cited papers directly compares direct structured answers, CoT, CoD, and Least-to-Most on a multi-specialist code-review pipeline with ground-truth defects.

- The evidence concerns generated reasoning text and, in some cases, reasoning models. It does not prescribe exposing private chain-of-thought in production artifacts. Final evidence and concise rationale can be evaluated independently.

- There is no established classifier that reliably decides when a code-review case needs CoT. The symbolic-versus-nonsymbolic distinction is a useful experimental stratification, not a complete routing algorithm. [To CoT or not to CoT?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.12183).

- CoD lacks broad replication and shows substantial zero-shot and small-model degradation. Least-to-Most lacks evidence that automatically generated decompositions generalize across review domains. [Chain of Draft](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.18600); [Least-to-Most](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.10625).

- The literature does not establish an optimal number of stages, reasoning tokens, draft words per step, or review rules. Those budgets must be selected from measured quality-cost curves, never treated as universal constants.
